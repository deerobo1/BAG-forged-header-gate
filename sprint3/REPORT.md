**The three conditions of the pass mark:**
1. Gate within 2 points of F: **7 of 7** (worst −0.72, IoT-23).
2. Gate beats B by ≥ 2 points: **3 of 7**.
3. Gate's header success ≤ B's: **7 of 7**. This holds by construction, unconditionally, since the gate clears a flow only if B does.

**Significance** (paired bootstrap with Holm correction across 7 runs, `summary_n2_holm.csv`):
- *Gate > B* is significant on IoT-23, USTC and TII on all three seeds; on MCFP for seeds 0 and 2 (seed 1: p_Holm = 0.056); and on 5GAD for seed 2.
- *Gate > F − 2* is significant on every run and seed. On MCFP the 95% interval of gate − F reaches down to −0.7.

**Gate against F.** On MCFP the gate *beats* F by 7.9–11.8 points: B catches malware that F calls Benign.

**Cost.** The gate never raises fewer false alarms than F. On the 3-seed means its Benign recall is 0.02 (USTC) to 3.86 (TII) points below F's: 2.05 on IoT-23, 1.32 on MCFP, and 0.06–0.21 elsewhere.

**Time split** (one seed, `summary_n2.csv`, setting `time0`): passes on 3 of 7 (CICIoT, IoT-23, USTC). On CICIoT, F and B both fall to about 50–55 macro-F1.

**Other wrapped detectors** (`results/e4_methods.csv`)
- *XGBoost.* XGBoost behind B (`xgb_gate`) keeps XGBoost's accuracy: 3-seed clean F1 92.8–99.9, against 93.6–100.0 alone. Its worst header success is 1.5% (unconditional, (a)). XGBoost alone: worst conditional header success, 3-seed mean, of 94–100% on 4 of 7 runs, 68–70% on TII and USTC, and 3.6% on CICIoT (`summary_n1.csv`). This is the strongest evidence that the wrapper isn't tied to one model.
- *Adversarial training* (`adv8`, seed 0 and time only): (b) still gets 63.6% (MCFP), 73.9% (MCFP strict) and 77.8% (MCFP, time) through. Gated by B, it drops to ≤ 0.43% on the random split and ≤ 5.1% on time (CICIoT).
- *AND of two plain models* (`and_plain`, seed 0): (b) gets 86–100% through on 4 of 7 runs.

## 4. Flags, outside M (E3)

- **Attack:** the free TCP flag bits (PSH, URG, ECE, CWR, AE and reserved) copied from donors with the same control bits, or best of 8 benign patterns.
- **Measured** (`results/summary_e3.csv`):
  - B alone lets at most 2.3% through on the random split; 37.5% on CICIoT's time split, where B already misses that much as recorded.
  - Gate F+B+C, flags then M-max: at most 0.25%.
  - The excess over the M-only bound is at most 0.05 points, in 0 of 28 settings above the pre-registered 1-point line.
- **Reading:** free flag bits are **outside the guarantee** (GUARANTEE.md §3), but in our data they are not a practical hole. The abstract must still say the guarantee covers M only.

## 5. Realizability (E5)

**R1: packet validity**
- 5 runs with raw pcaps (TII has none), 300 forged flows and 1,500 forged packets each.
- 0 malformed packets and 0 IP or TCP checksum errors, from each of three independent validators: our RFC 1071 checks, dpkt and scapy (`results/e5_forge.csv`).
- For context, 1,500 of 1,500 *original* 5GAD packets and 107 USTC packets had bad TCP checksums as captured, typical of checksum offload. The forged ones are correct.
- tshark wasn't available (`BLOCKERS.md` 3).

**R2 and R3: live sessions**
- 250 sessions, between two network namespaces, with an unmodified Linux receiver (`results/e5_live.csv`).
- 250 of 250 handshakes completed with the forged SYN values on the wire.
- 243 of 250 echoed data back. The 7 failures (all 5GAD) are fully explained by copied TCP timestamps: 0 of 7 succeed when re-run with them, 7 of 7 without (`results/e5_live_retry.json`).

**Classes** (`results/e5_realizability.csv`)
- **Free:** TTL, TOS, DF, ISN, window range.
- **At a cost:** IP ID, exact window values, window scale and timestamp options.
- **Not realizable:** none of the values used.

So attack success restricted to the realizable classes is **unchanged** from the full numbers.

**Placement on our scale** (Alatawi's taxonomy is UNVERIFIED):
- (a), (b), (m): R1 for all values; R2 and R3 for the replayed value sets, except copied timestamps, which need the attacker's stack at both ends.

**What breaks C is chaff.**
- Chaff is the worst held-out attack on all three failing runs.

**Do not claim:**
1. Detection inside encryption. The "encrypted" attack flows are mostly a plaintext ClientHello (memory note, Oct 31).
2. A guarantee for anything outside M: packet count, sizes, payload, timing, flags, options length, fragmentation.
3. That C makes the detector robust to packet attacks, or to chaff in particular.
4. That we're the first to forge header fields against packet- or byte-level classifiers. FlowPaint (arXiv 2606.22717) does it header-only with checksum repair, for censorship; Adversarial Pre-Padding (arXiv 2510.25810) does it with inserted bytes. Don't quote FlowPaint's numbers without the full text (only the first 100k characters were read).
5. That we're the first, or the strongest, at packet-insertion robustness for raw-byte models (CertTA). We didn't compare with CertTA or BARS.
6. "50–100% on seven datasets": there are six datasets, and USTC is 1.9–32.7%.
7. LitCVit results on more than 2 runs.
8. That masking loses clean accuracy in general. It does on 3 of 7 runs here, and barely on flow features in elShehaby and Matrawy.
9. "At most 1 point of extra false alarms" relative to the full model. That holds against the two-model gate (C's own cost: ≤ 0.73 on the 3-seed means), but the gate costs up to 3.86 points against F.
10. "At most 0.2% cleared on every run, seed and split" for the two-model gate (30.3% on CICIoT's time split).
11. Any time-split number as final (one seed).
12. Source-disjoint or host-independent generalisation. Most malware classes come from 1–2 hosts.
13. Deployability on real traffic.
14. Anything from Singh et al. or Alatawi (UNVERIFIED).

**What to emphasise, ranked by evidence:**
1. **The shortcut fields are attacker-settable, and forging them breaks undefended raw-byte detectors.** 7 runs, 3 seeds, 2 model families plus LitCVit on 2 runs; realizability checked at packet level and in live sessions (L1–L6).
2. **A header-blind member gives a checked bound on header attacks.** 0 invariance failures and 0 bound violations; ≤ 0.25% with the three-model gate everywhere. It works around XGBoost too, and two-model ensembles and adversarial training don't match it (L7–L10, L15, L16).
3. **The accuracy trade-off, stated narrowly.** The gate recovers 9–51 points over masking on the three runs where masking hurts, and costs up to 3.9 points of Benign recall (L11, L12, L14).

4. **A dataset finding that affects every paper on these benchmarks, LitCVit's included.** The detectors recognise benign hosts, not benign behaviour (L21). This belongs in the evaluation section and limitations. It could replace the chaff sentence in the security abstract if the venue values evaluation findings: "With benign hosts held out, benign recall falls to 7–34% on four runs" adds about 14 words.

Packet-level robustness (C) belongs in the limitations (L18, L19).

## 10. Related work (≤ 200 words; only papers we read)

> Raw-byte traffic classifiers learn shortcuts: header fields such as TTL, checksums, window, sequence numbers and timestamps (Wang et al., arXiv 2601.10180; Wickramasinghe et al., arXiv 2503.20093), whose effect depends on the split (Yu et al., arXiv 2610.17961). None of these works considers an attacker. Others forge such fields to evade classifiers: FlowPaint (arXiv 2606.22717) edits IP ID, window, urgent pointer and TOS against nPrint-based censors, and Adversarial Pre-Padding (arXiv 2510.25810) edits TCP fields together with inserted bytes against ET-BERT and YaTC. Neither proposes a defence. We study malware detectors, including LitCVit, and check realizability on real packets and live sessions. Restricting a detector to features the attacker can't change goes back to monotonic classifiers (Incer et al., IWSPA 2018) and conserved features (Tong et al., USENIX Security 2019). elShehaby and Matrawy mask easily perturbed flow features (arXiv 2409.07448). Certified defences cover ℓp perturbations (BARS, NDSS 2023) and packet insertion, padding and delay (CertTA, USENIX Security 2025). Our packet-level view is empirical, and chaff (NetMasquerade, arXiv 2510.14906) defeats it on three runs.

## 11. Abstracts

**Security venue (≤ 180 words)**

> Raw-byte malicious-traffic detectors read IP and TCP header fields that recent work calls shortcuts. The sender sets these fields. Forging only them (TTL, IP ID, type of service, window, TCP option values, initial sequence numbers), with checksums recomputed and payload and lengths unchanged, makes a byte-image detector call at least 59% of detected malware benign on six of seven dataset runs, in each of three seeds. A LitCVit rebuild is fooled on 58–82% of flows in both runs tested. Forged packets pass three independent validators, and 250 of 250 forged handshakes complete against an unmodified Linux stack. We wrap the detector in a gate that clears a flow only if a model blind to the declared fields agrees. Attack success is then bounded by that model's miss rate, which we confirm byte by byte on 649,966 attacked flows. The gate stays within 0.72 macro-F1 of the full model, recovers 9–51 points over masking where masking hurts, and costs up to 3.9 points of benign recall. Chaff packets still evade it on three runs.

**Vision and machine-intelligence venue (≤ 180 words)**

This framing is honest only as *shortcut learning in image-encoded inputs*. The models are image CNNs on 40×40 byte images, and the finding is that some pixels are attacker-controlled. Nothing here is a contribution to visual recognition. If the venue requires visual data, don't submit there.

> Byte-image classifiers render each network flow as a 40×40 image and reach high accuracy on malware-traffic benchmarks. We show that the pixels they rely on most are ones an adversary controls: the header bytes that encode TTL, identification, window and option values. Rewriting only those pixels, in ways real network stacks accept (250 of 250 live handshakes complete), makes a MobileNetV2 call at least 59% of the malware it detected benign on six of seven dataset runs and three seeds. We propose a two-view decision rule: the full image may only be believed when a second network, which never sees the attacker's pixels, agrees. This bounds the success of any such pixel-level attack by the second network's miss rate. We check the bound exactly on 649,966 attacked images. The two-view rule stays within 0.72 macro-F1 of the full-image model and recovers 9–51 points over masking the pixels on the three runs where masking hurts, at a cost of up to 3.9 points of benign recall. Attacks that insert whole rows (packets) remain open.
