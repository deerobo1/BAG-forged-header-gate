# Sprint report (Nov 3, 2026)

All numbers come from `sprint/results/` (raw CSV and JSON from the Kaggle jobs, under `kaggle/<setting>/`). They were scored by `sprint/verdicts.py` against the pass marks fixed in `01_preregistration.md` (commits 5d17b06 and 15a043e, both made before any run). The tables are regenerated by `python sprint/verdicts.py && python sprint/report_tables.py`, and the LaTeX versions are in `sprint/tables/`.

**Settings:**
- Random split, seeds 0, 1 and 2, and the split by time (seed 0); all 7 runs each.
- Seed 0 and the time split ran every idea.
- Seeds 1 and 2 ran the "light" job only: the baselines, the length view and the analyses, with no neural training. That covers every idea that reached MAYBE or better on seed 0 without training.

"Let through" is the notes' definition: of the malware flows the attack changed that the method detects when clean, the share it calls Benign. The gate is F (full view) plus B (shortcut fields zeroed). A flow is cleared only if both say Benign.

## 2. Main result: the length view (1C)

C is an XGBoost model on 41 header-blind features. For each of the first 5 packets it reads: present, IP version, protocol, IP header length, total length, TCP data offset, TCP flags and payload length. It also reads the total payload kept. `C_A` is trained with one added-packet copy of each malware flow. The gate with C clears a flow only if F, B and C all say Benign.

- **Cost.** Over all 4 settings, at most 0.95 points of Benign recall and at most 2.02 points of macro-F1. The 41-byte rule costs 34.7–38.0 points of Benign recall on USTC.

## 4. Claims

**Supported**
- The gate keeps header attacks closed on every run, seed and split. With C, (a) lets at most 0.11% through and (b) at most 0.17%.
- Its guarantee is structural for the shortcut fields, because B cannot see them: certified is the share of malware B flags.
- The gain comes from header blindness, not from ensembling or adversarial training (section 3).
- A header-blind length view trained with added packets closes the packet attacks on CICIoT, IoT-23 and MCFP strict, and on MCFP on the split by time. It costs at most 0.95 points of Benign recall. It generalises to the held-out split family (at most 1.67%) and, on these runs, to unseen attack parameters.
- The gate wraps another detector: XGBoost behind B lets at most 0.9% through on (a) and (b).

**Not supported**
- Any guarantee against packet or payload attacks. C covers them only empirically, against our attack suite.
- Robustness on 5GAD. The adaptive attacker gets 45–92% through with C_A, and up to 99.6% with unseen parameters. On MCFP's random split, 15–32% gets through.
- A coverage guarantee under drift (2B on the time split).
- That abstention helps under attack, beyond fewer false alerts on clean traffic.
- Anything about timing features: the data has no per-packet timestamps.
- Any attack that changes the application byte stream. Inserting new payload isn't modelled.

## 5. Failures and bugs found

1. **Conformal with an empty calibration half.** With no malware to calibrate on, the threshold became −inf and reported a meaningless 100% coverage (5GAD and MCFP strict, time split, rolling). Fixed in `analysis_saved.py`, which now returns NaN and records n_cal. Seed 0 and the time split ran before the fix, so their raw files still contain those two rows. The tables mark them "not computable".
2. **5GAD conformal below target on random halves (92.1, 92.8).** Not explained. B's scores are saturated near 0 (thresholds around −5e−5), so ties are a likely cause, but this wasn't checked.
3. **The noisy-AND worst-of-K training collapsed:** macro-F1 fell 36–49 points and Benign recall up to 56 points. The plain noisy-AND loss costs 4.9–7.5 points of F1. Its weights were fixed in advance with no tuning, so a tuned version might do better. It wasn't tried.
4. **The 41-byte rule** costs 34.7–38.0 points of Benign recall on USTC, which hadn't been scored before. It also fails the adaptive attacker on 5GAD and MCFP strict (46–48% on seed 0).
5. **Deviations from the brief, all in the pre-registration:** 1B trains B only; worst-of-K uses a hard max; 1C uses XGBoost; trained ideas ran on seed 0 and the time split only (seeds 1 and 2 got no neural training).

**Not tested:**
- the timing view (no data)
- Tier 3 (Vision Mamba, EdgeNeXt)
- 1B with F trained too
- the tuned noisy-AND loss

## 6. Recommendations

1. **Make the method a three-view gate: F, B, and C trained on added packets.** Use `C_A` rather than `C_AS`: same verdict, a simpler training recipe, and its held-out result on split payloads is the generalisation evidence. Report 3-seed means and the time split, as in section 2.
2. **Split the claims in two.** For header fields, a structural guarantee from B. For packet attacks, empirical robustness from C against a stated suite, with 5GAD and MCFP (random split) as named limitations. Drop the 41-byte rule and conformal coverage under drift from the claims. Keep conformal on the random split as a side result, with its exceptions.
3. **Put the reviewer baselines in the paper** (AND of two plain models; adversarial training, ungated and gated). They answer the two obvious objections, "it's just an ensemble" and "just train adversarially", with numbers.

**Cheapest next experiment (CPU, minutes, no new training):** find out why C fails on 5GAD. From the saved predictions, break (g)'s successes down by class and by attack setting (operation, copies, position, split size), and compare C's features for evaded malware against the benign flows. Then we'll know whether 5GAD's malware becomes indistinguishable from benign by length, which would be a limit worth stating, or whether C is missing a feature.

## 7. Follow-up: why the length view fails on 5GAD (done Nov 3, no training)

`sprint/diag_5gad.py` replays the adaptive attacker's random settings from its seed and lines them up with the Kaggle job's saved predictions. The replay reproduces each job's reported (g) number exactly: 45.24 (seed 0, C_A), 34.96 (seed 0, C_AS) and 92.11 (seed 1, C_A). Raw output: `sprint/results/diag_5gad2022_*.json`.

- **C is the bottleneck.** Per attempt on 5GAD malware, F says Benign 56–73% of the time and B 72%, but C_A only 20%. So one attempt clears all three about 4% of the time, and the best of 16 attempts gets 45–92% through.
- **5GAD's malware differs from benign in one number in the first 5 packets.** Every class shares the TCP handshake. What tells them apart is the payload length of the 4th packet: 142–268 bytes for malware, against 517, 583 or 64 for the common benign flows.
- **Two tricks hide that number:**
  1. **Split the payload into 16- or 32-byte segments.** This erases the length and leaves a pattern seen in no training flow, benign or malware. It's the most common cleared pattern on seed 1 (707 and 601 cleared attempts). C_A never trained on splits, and C_AS trained only on sizes 1, 2 and 4.
  2. **Repeat the SYN 3 times** ("SYN SYN SYN SYN SYN-ACK"). This pattern appears in exactly one of 4,258 benign training flows and in no malware flow. It is the only hole left for C_AS on seed 0 (all 334 cleared attempts), and it needs 3 copies, outside C_AS's training range of 1–2.
- **So 5GAD is not a case of indistinguishable traffic.** The cleared flows match no malware flow, and benign traffic almost never looks like them. They are unfamiliar patterns that C's trees send to Benign.
- **An exact-match rule fails as a fix.** "Never clear a flow whose length pattern wasn't seen in benign training" would wrongly flag 3.9% of benign test flows on 5GAD, but 10.6% on CICIoT and 16.6% on MCFP and MCFP strict (seed 0). That's too many false alarms.

**Next step that follows (exploratory, not pre-registered):** train C with augmentation that covers the attacker's whole range: copies 1–3, any position, split sizes 1–32. Then test it only on the unseen-parameter attacker (u), or on a new held-out size such as 24. This is an XGBoost fit on a CPU, minutes per run. If it closes 5GAD while (u) stays closed, the paper can say so. If not, 5GAD goes in the limitations with the explanation above.
